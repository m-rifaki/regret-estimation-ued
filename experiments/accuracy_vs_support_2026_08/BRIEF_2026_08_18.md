# MCTS-UED, 2026-08-18

Six words used throughout, so nothing below needs unpacking:

- **level** = one task. Here, one goal square in a maze.
- **best-possible score** = the score a perfect player would get on that level. We can
  compute it exactly, because the maze is small. It is the only number in the system that
  ever has to be estimated.
- **solver** = the thing that estimates it. PPO and two kinds of tree search.
- **regret** = best-possible score minus what the student currently gets.
- **curriculum** = which levels the student trains on. Here, probability proportional to
  regret. One line, no parameters, so it is never the thing at fault.
- **clamp** = a one-line fix: never report a best-possible score below 0.01.

---

## What August 6 found

I trained a student under six different estimates of the best-possible score, and measured
what it could do at the end.

One thing predicted the whole outcome: **whether the solver reported a number above zero.**
Not how close that number was. Across 25 combinations of method and level, "what fraction
of runs reported something above zero" predicted the final score to within half a point.

Two things followed, and both looked like the end of the project:

1. Being accurate bought nothing. One solver was wrong by a factor of 269 and scored 100%.
2. The clamp was free. It took PPO from 8% to 99.5%, which is what the expensive solver
   also gets.

If one line of code buys what a better solver buys, there is no reason to study solvers.

## What that left open

August 6 answered "what happened". It did not answer "why", and it did not answer whether
that was a fact about regret estimation in general or a fact about that particular setup:
five levels, all of them solvable, 1800 practice episodes.

So I ran two things. First, why did it happen. Second, does it still happen when the setup
is less forgiving.

---

## Figure 1. Why being accurate bought nothing

![Figure 1](figures/fig_mechanism_trajectories.png)

**Why this figure exists.** August 6 said accuracy did not matter. This says why, using
per-round data those same runs recorded and that I had never plotted.

**How to read it.** Five panels, one per solver. Each thin line is one run. The line shows
how likely the curriculum was to pick the hardest level, at each of 100 rounds. Thick black
is the average of the twelve runs. Under each panel heading is the number that solver
reported, against a true value of 0.673.

**What it shows.** The solver reporting 0.0025, which is 269 times too small, ends up giving
the hardest level a 0.92 chance. The solver reporting the correct 0.673 gives it 0.67. The
wrong one concentrates harder than the right one.

**Why.** The curriculum divides each level's regret by the total. Once the easy levels are
learned their regret is zero, so whatever is left takes everything, no matter how small it
is. You can watch that happen between rounds 5 and 20. The size of the number never enters
the calculation. Zero is the only value that behaves differently, and the last two panels
show it: report zero and the level is never picked again.

## Figure 2. Why that setup could not have shown anything else

![Figure 2](figures/fig_competition.png)

**Why this figure exists.** If the curriculum collapses onto one level, then of course the
size of the estimate is irrelevant. So the question becomes: how many levels are actually
in play at once, and what sets that number.

**How to read it.** Three panels, 5, 15 and 30 levels, all with the same 1800 episodes and
so 18 episodes per round. Each thin line is one run, showing how many levels the curriculum
still gives any chance to.

**What it shows.** With 5 levels it ends with about 2 in play, so each gets about 8.6
episodes a round. With 30 it ends with about 20 in play, so each gets about 0.9.

**Why this is the point.** With two candidates and 18 episodes you can afford both, so their
order does not change anything. With twenty candidates and 18 episodes you cannot, so the
order decides who gets taught. August 6 was in the first situation.

## Figure 3. With 30 levels, knowing the order does matter

![Figure 3](figures/fig_mastery_matrix.png)

**Why this figure exists.** To test the order directly. I shuffled the exact best-possible
scores between levels. Same set of numbers, all still above zero, and the
only thing broken is which level gets which. So the single thing being tested is whether the
curriculum knows which levels are hard.

**How to read it.** One square is one level in one run. Twelve rows, one per run, and a row
is the same maze and the same starting seed in all three panels. Thirty columns, easiest
level on the left. Dark green means the student learned that level, finishing at 100%. Pale orange means
it got partly there. Grey means it never got there. Third panel is the baseline: ignore regret and sample every level
equally.

**What it shows.** 126 levels learned out of 360 when the order is right. 101 when the
order is shuffled. 104 with no curriculum at all.

**Say this before he does.** The shuffled curriculum is not better than no curriculum here.
So the honest claim is not "curricula help". It is "this curriculum helps when it knows the
order, and is worth nothing when it does not". Also, the right-hand half of every panel is
grey: with 1800 episodes nothing ever reaches the hardest fifteen levels. The order decides
how far into the middle you get.

---

## Figure 4. And it is the practice budget that decides

![Figure 4](figures/fig_ranking_matters.png)

**Why this figure exists.** Two things changed between 5 levels and 30: how many levels
compete, and how much practice each one gets. This separates them. It is the figure that
stops the obvious objection.

**How to read it.** One dot is one run: its score with the correct order minus its score
with the shuffled order, on the same maze with the same seed. Above the grey line, knowing
the order helped. Black bar is the average of twelve. Left panel holds the total practice at
1800 episodes, so each level gets less as levels are added. Right panel grows the budget so
every level always gets 360 episodes.

**What it shows.** On the left the gap opens: 11 of 12 runs favour the correct order at 15
levels, 10 of 12 at 30. On the right it does not open at all: 6 of 12 at 30 levels, which is
a coin toss.

**So the condition is a practice budget too small to cover the levels.** Not the number of
levels, and not how hard they are.

## Figure 5. What the clamp actually costs

![Figure 5](figures/fig_where_training_went.png)

**Why this figure exists.** On August 6 the clamp looked free. Every level there was
solvable, so promising that no level is worthless costs nothing. Here two of seven levels
are made impossible to reach, so their true best-possible score is exactly zero and any
practice spent on them is thrown away.

**How to read it.** One square is one level in one run. Six panels, one per solver. Seven
columns: five reachable levels, then the two impossible ones to the right of the blue line.
Colour is how much of that run's practice went to that level.

**What it shows.** Above the top row the region right of the blue line is pale: 0% for the
exact scores, 0% for the good tree search. In the two clamped panels the colour runs
straight through the line. Both spend about 40% of all practice on levels nobody can ever
solve.

**Say all of this before he does.** The clamp is still worth using here. It throws away 40%
of practice and still gains 23 to 33 points, because a curriculum stuck at zero is worse
than a wasteful one. And the clamped weak search scores 61% against the good search's 50%,
so the good search does not win on score. Only the exact scores are good on both counts, at
65% and 0%.

## Figure 6. Being half right about the order is worth nothing

![Figure 6](figures/fig_partial_ordering.png)

**Why this figure exists.** Figures 3 and 4 compare a perfect order against a broken one.
That leaves open whether a solver that gets partway there gets part of the benefit. It
matters, because "make the solver somewhat better" is the whole of Step 2.

**How to read it.** One dot is one run. Left to right is how well that run's estimates put
the levels in the true order, from 0 for no information to 1 for exactly right. Up and down
is that run's score minus the no-curriculum score on the same maze and seed, so the red line
means no better than not bothering.

**What it shows.** At 30 levels the exactly-correct order gains 7.1 points, and every broken
version gains 2.3 to 4.1. The broken ones cannot be told apart from each other: half right
does no better than completely wrong.

**So it is a step, not a slope.** Partly improving a solver appears to buy nothing.

## Where this leaves the project

Solver quality is worth something under two conditions, and both were absent on August 6:

1. **The practice budget cannot cover the levels.** Then the order matters.
2. **Some levels are genuinely impossible.** Then promising every level is worth something
   costs 40% of practice, and a solver that knows the difference does not pay it.

The thing worth having is not accuracy and not a floor. It is a solver whose zero means
something. The clamp claims no level is ever worthless. A good solver knows which ones are.

## Three things to decide with him

1. Is "a zero that means something" the Step 2 claim? If yes, the next thing to build is a
   floor applied only where the solver is unsure, never where it confidently says zero.
2. Which definition of the best-possible score counts as regret. Still open from July 16,
   and it sets the target for everything in jaxued.
3. What number of levels to use in jaxued. Five would land back in the setting where the
   order provably does not matter. Fifteen is the cheapest one where it does.

## If he pushes

**"Why a maze and not Overcooked?"** Picking a partner in Overcooked is a one-shot choice,
which was his own point in June, so a planning search has nothing to plan over. And the true
best-possible score is not computable there, so no claim about a solver could be graded.

**"Is this one lucky maze?"** No. At 30 levels all four mazes point the same way, and
dropping any single maze still leaves the effect positive, worst case 2.4 points. One maze is
about three times the others, so the size varies and I cannot say why with four mazes.

**"So does the good tree search win?"** Not on score once impossible levels exist. The
clamped weak search beats it, 61% against 50%. It wins on not wasting practice, at 0%. Only
the exact scores win on both. I have no explanation for its score shortfall and I am not
going to invent one.

**"It is all a small maze with a lookup-table student."** Yes. Four mazes, three seeds,
no neural network. Putting a value network in and moving to jaxued is the next work and
neither is done.

**"Did you check the numbers?"** Every number here is recomputed from the raw results by a
script, 180 checks. It found three of my mistakes, the real one being a range I had read off
a chart by eye instead of computing.
