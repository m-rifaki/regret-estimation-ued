# MCTS-UED, 2026-08-18

## August 6

- One student, six different estimates of each level's best-possible score, everything
  else identical. Measured the final score on every level.
- One thing predicted every outcome: whether the estimate was above zero. Across 25
  method-and-level pairs it predicted the final score within 0.5 points.
- Closeness did not matter. One solver was badly wrong and still scored 100%.
- The clamp took PPO from 8% to 99.5%, equal to the expensive solver.
- Read straight, that ends the project: one line of code buys what a better solver buys.
- Untested: that run had 5 levels, every one possible, 1800 practice episodes.

## What was left

1. Why did closeness not matter?
2. Does it still not matter when the setup is harder?

Figures 1 and 2 answer the first. Figures 3, 4 and 6 answer the second. Figure 5 tests
the clamp.

## Figure 1

![Figure 1](figures/fig_mechanism_trajectories.png)

- **Asks:** did a bigger estimate concentrate the curriculum more?
- **Changed:** nothing. This is per-round data the August 6 runs already recorded.
- **One mark:** one thin line is one run, black is their mean; height is the chance the
  curriculum picks the hardest level that round. The dotted line at 0.2 is an even split
  over 5 levels. Each panel heading says only how wrong that solver's guess was; no guess
  is plotted.
- **Result:** count the lines. In the first three panels 11 of 12 runs concentrate on the
  hardest level. In the fourth only 1 of 12 concentrates. In the fifth none do, and all 12 stay
  flat at zero.
- **The bit you cannot see:** how good each guess was is not on this figure. The solver in
  panel 3 was badly wrong, the one in panel 1 was exactly right, and their pictures are
  nearly the same. Panel 3 even ends highest. Say that out loud before he asks, because the
  plot alone will not tell him.
- **Means:** the curriculum divides each regret by the total, so once the easy levels are
  learned whatever is left takes the whole thing, however small it is. Zero is the only value that acts
  differently, because zero regret is never picked again.

## Figure 2

![Figure 2](figures/fig_competition.png)

- **Asks:** how many levels are in play at once, and what sets that number.
- **Changed:** the number of levels, 5 to 15 to 30, with practice fixed at 1800 episodes.
- **One mark:** one thin line is one run, black is their mean; height is how many levels
  the curriculum still gives any chance.
- **Result:** 5 levels end with 2 still in play, so 8.6 episodes each per round. 30 levels
  end with 20 in play, so 0.9 each.
- **Means:** with 2 in play and 18 episodes you cover both, so the order changes nothing.
  With 20 you cannot, so the order decides who is taught. August 6 was the first case.

## Figure 3

![Figure 3](figures/fig_mastery_matrix.png)

- **Asks:** with the same practice, does knowing the order matter?
- **Changed:** the exact best-possible scores were shuffled between levels. Same numbers,
  all still above zero, only which level gets which is broken.
- **One mark:** one square is one level in one run, easiest level on the left; the twelve
  rows are the twelve runs, and a row is the same maze and seed in all three panels. Green means learned, orange partly there, grey
  never got there.
- **Result:** 126 levels learned of 360 with the exact order, 101 shuffled, 104 with no
  curriculum.
- **Means:** the shuffled curriculum is no better than no curriculum, so the value is in
  knowing the order, not in having a curriculum. The grey right half is levels nothing
  reaches in 1800 episodes.

## Figure 4

![Figure 4](figures/fig_ranking_matters.png)

- **Asks:** does knowing which levels are hardest make the student better, and is it the
  practice budget or the number of levels that decides?
- **Changed:** the left panel keeps total practice at 1800, so each level gets fewer
  episodes as levels are added. The right panel gives 360 episodes per level throughout.
- **One mark:** one dot is one run, black bar their mean: its score with the exact order
  minus its score with the shuffled order, same maze, same seed.
- **Result:** left, 11 of 12 runs favour the exact order at 15 levels and 10 of 12 at 30.
  Right, 6 of 12 at 30, a coin toss.
- **Means:** the condition is practice too short to cover the levels. Not their number,
  and not their difficulty.

## Figure 5

![Figure 5](figures/fig_where_training_went.png)

- **Asks:** what does the clamp cost when a level is impossible?
- **Changed:** two of seven levels made unreachable, so their true best-possible score is
  exactly zero and any practice there is wasted.
- **One mark:** one square is one level in one run; colour is the share of that run's
  practice.
- **Result:** the exact scores and the good tree search send 0% past the blue line. Both
  clamped versions send about 40%.
- **Means:** the clamp promises no level is worthless, which is false here, and it pays
  40% of practice for the promise. It still gains 23 to 33 points, because a curriculum
  stuck at zero is worse than a wasteful one. The clamped weak search scores 61% against
  the good search's 50%, so the good search does not win on score. Only the exact scores
  are good on both counts, 65% and 0%.

## Figure 6

![Figure 6](figures/fig_partial_ordering.png)

- **Asks:** does a partly correct order help, or only an exactly correct one?
- **Changed:** four ways of degrading the order, from mild noise to fully shuffled.
- **One mark:** one dot is one run, black bar their mean; across is how well it put the
  levels in order, 0 no information and 1 exactly right, up is its score minus the
  no-curriculum score on the same maze and seed. Blue is the exact order, green a damaged
  one.
- **Result:** at 30 levels the exact order gains 7.1 points and every degraded version
  gains 2.3 to 4.1, with the degraded ones indistinguishable from each other.
- **Means:** half right is worth the same as wrong. Partly improving a solver buys
  nothing, which is a harder requirement than Step 2 assumed.
